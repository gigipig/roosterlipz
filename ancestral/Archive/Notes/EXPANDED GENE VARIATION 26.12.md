# Population-Specific Gene Variants for Dietary Genetic Adaptations

Genetic adaptations to diet represent some of the strongest signals of natural selection in the human genome. This report documents **11 key genes** with population-specific variants across ancestral regions, providing rs numbers, population distributions, and peer-reviewed citations from landmark studies including Tishkoff et al. 2007, Perry et al. 2007, and Fumagalli et al. 2015.

---

## LCT (Lactase Persistence)

Lactase persistence evolved independently at least five times across human populations, representing a textbook example of convergent evolution driven by dairy pastoralism beginning **~7,000-9,000 years ago**.

### European populations

* **rs4988235 (−13910 C>T)** — Found in Finnish (59%), Northern Europeans from Utah (74%), British (72%), Germans, Dutch, Scandinavians. Selection coefficient s=0.10 in Finnish; originated ~9,100 years ago. Also present in Central Asians (Kazakhs 85-92%) and South Asians (10-18%) via genetic diffusion.
  * Enattah et al. 2002, *Nature Genetics* 30:233-237 (PMID: 11788828)
  * Bersaglieri et al. 2004, *Am J Hum Genet* 74:1111-1120 (PMID: 15114531)

### East African populations

* **rs145946881 (−14010 G>C)** — Found in Maasai (Kenya/Tanzania), Nilotic Tanzanians and Kenyans. Selection coefficient s=0.10; originated ~4,900 years ago. Extended haplotype homozygosity >2 Mb indicates recent strong selection.
  * Tishkoff et al. 2007, *Nature Genetics* 39:31-40 (PMID: 17159977)
  * Ranciaro et al. 2014, *Am J Hum Genet* 94:496-510 (PMID: 24630847)

* **rs41525747 (−13907 C>G)** — Found in Ethiopian (Amhara, Oromo), Sudanese (Beja, Nubian; up to 34%), and some Saudi populations.
  * Ingram et al. 2007, *Hum Genet* 120:779-788 (PMID: 17120047)
  * Tishkoff et al. 2007, *Nature Genetics* 39:31-40 (PMID: 17159977)

### Middle Eastern/Arabian populations

* **rs41380347 (−13915 T>G)** — Found in Bedouin Arabs (Saudi Arabia), Rashaayda (eastern Sahel; 76.5%), Jordanian Bedouins, and some East/North African populations. Originated ~7,700 years ago.
  * Enattah et al. 2008, *Am J Hum Genet* 82:57-72 (PMID: 18179885)
  * Imtiaz et al. 2007, *J Med Genet* 44:e89 (PMID: 17911653)

### Northeast African populations

* **rs869051967 (−14009 T>G)** — Found in Sudanese Arab, Nubian, and Beja populations.
  * Ingram et al. 2009, *Hum Genet* 125:315-324 (PMID: 19242650)
  * Hollfelder et al. 2021, *Genome Biol Evol* (PMC8175049)

## AMY1 (Amylase/Starch Digestion)

Unlike other dietary adaptations driven by SNPs, AMY1 variation is primarily **copy number variation (CNV)**, ranging from 2-20 diploid copies. Higher copy numbers correlate with populations historically consuming starch-rich diets.

### High-starch agricultural populations (mean ≥6 copies)

* **Japanese** — Mean ~7-8 copies; agricultural diet for >3,000 years
* **European-Americans** — Mean ~7 copies; agricultural ancestry
* **Hadza (Tanzania)** — Mean ~7 copies despite hunter-gatherer lifestyle; root/tuber-based diet

### Low-starch pastoral/hunter-gatherer populations (mean ~5 copies)

* **Biaka Pygmies (Central African Republic)** — Mean ~5 copies; rainforest hunter-gatherers
* **Mbuti Pygmies (Democratic Republic of Congo)** — Mean ~5 copies; rainforest diet
* **Datog (Tanzania)** — Mean ~5 copies; pastoralists with low starch consumption
* **Yakut (Siberia)** — Mean ~5 copies; fishing/pastoralist subsistence

**Key finding:** 70% of high-starch population individuals carry ≥6 AMY1 copies versus only 37% in low-starch populations. Japanese-Yakut differentiation represents a significant outlier in genome-wide CNV patterns.

### Associated regulatory SNP

* **rs11185098** — Located in AMY1-AMY2 region; A allele indicates higher amylase activity. Used as proxy for copy number in GWAS studies.
  * Perry et al. 2007, *Nature Genetics* 39:1256-1260 (PMID: 17828263)
  * Carpenter et al. 2015, *Hum Mol Genet* 24:3472-3480 (PMID: 25788522)

## FADS1 (Fatty Acid Desaturase)

The FADS gene cluster shows remarkable population-specific selection, with **opposite selection pressures** in populations consuming plant-based diets versus marine-based diets.

### African populations (near-fixation of derived allele)

* **rs174537 G allele** — 81% GG homozygous in African Americans; essentially fixed in sub-Saharan Africans. Selection began 202,000-492,000 years ago for increased LC-PUFA synthesis on plant-based diets.
  * Mathias et al. 2012, *PLoS ONE* 7:e44926 (PMID: 22984582)

* **rs174546 C allele** — 95-98% derived allele in African populations.
  * Ameur et al. 2012, *Am J Hum Genet* 90:809-820 (PMID: 22503634)

### European populations (recent strong selection)

* **rs174546 C allele** — ~64% derived allele; increased from near 0% in Mesolithic Europeans to present frequency. Selection began 1,700-4,000 years ago with s=3.4-6.9%.
  * Mathieson et al. 2015, *Nature* 528:499-503 (PMID: 26595274)
  * Mathieson & Mathieson 2018, *Mol Biol Evol* 35:2957-2970 (PMID: 30272210)

### Greenlandic Inuit/Arctic populations (opposite selection direction)

* **rs174570 T allele** — 98-100% in Greenlandic Inuit versus only 16% in Europeans. Top selection signal in Inuit; adaptation to marine diet reduces endogenous LC-PUFA synthesis.
  * Fumagalli et al. 2015, *Science* 349:1343-1347 (PMID: 26383953)

* **rs7115739** — Nearly fixed derived allele in Greenlandic Inuit; associated with −2 cm height and −4 kg weight.
  * Fumagalli et al. 2015, *Science* 349:1343-1347 (PMID: 26383953)

### South Asian populations (vegetarian diet adaptation)

* **rs66698963 (22bp insertion)** — ~70% insertion allele in South Asians versus ~29% in East Asians. Positive selection for increased FADS activity associated with vegetarian diets.
  * Kothapalli et al. 2016, *Mol Biol Evol* 33:1726-1739 (PMID: 26931177)

### Native American populations

* **rs174546** — Ancestral allele nearly fixed (>99%). Recent research suggests frequency patterns reflect ancestral Northern Eurasian frequencies at time of American colonization.
  * Mathieson 2020, *Mol Biol Evol* 37:2029-2033 (PMID: 32145021)

## ADH1B (Alcohol Metabolism)

ADH1B variants affect ethanol oxidation rate and show strong population-specific selection, potentially linked to rice domestication in East Asia.

### East Asian populations

* **rs1229984 (Arg48His, ADH1B*2)** — Japanese: 70-81% carriers (34-71% homozygous); Korean: 88-96% carriers (50-65% homozygous); Han Chinese: 70-92% carriers; Vietnamese: 69%. The variant increases ethanol metabolism **70-80 fold** and protects against alcoholism.
  * Li et al. 2008, *PLoS ONE* 3(4):e1881 (PMID: 18382665)
  * Peng et al. 2010, *BMC Evol Biol* 10:15 (PMID: 20089146)

### Southeast Asian populations

* **rs1229984** — Thai: 54% carriers (13% homozygous); Filipino: 80% carriers (40% homozygous); Malay: 83% carriers (35% homozygous). Lower homozygosity than Northeast Asians.
  * Eng et al. 2007, *Alcohol Res Health* 30(1):22-27 (PMID: 17718397)

### African populations

* **rs2066702 (Arg370Cys, ADH1B*3)** — African-specific variant with **3× faster** ethanol metabolism. MAF: African Americans 19%, Ugandans 18%, varies 9-28% across West/East African populations. Virtually absent in Europeans and East Asians.
  * Polimanti & Gelernter 2018, *Am J Med Genet B* (PMC5617762)

### European populations

* **rs1229984** — MAF only 3-5%; ~90% carry slow-metabolizing ADH1B*1 genotype.

## ALDH2 (Alcohol Metabolism)

### East Asian populations (causes "Asian flush")

* **rs671 (Glu504Lys, ALDH2*2)** — Affects ~540 million East Asians (8% of world population). Causes acetaldehyde accumulation leading to facial flushing, nausea, and tachycardia after alcohol consumption.

| Population | Carriers | Homozygous (*2/*2) |
|------------|----------|-------------------|
| Japanese | ~46% | 6% |
| Han Chinese (Taiwan) | ~55% | 8% |
| Han Chinese (Northern) | ~22% | 2% |
| Korean | ~30-34% | 3% |
| Vietnamese | ~20% | 2.7% |
| Thai | ~10% | 0% |
| Filipino | ~1% | 0% |

The variant originated ~2,000-3,000 years ago from a single founder event. It is **virtually absent** in European, African, and South Asian populations.
  * Eng et al. 2007, *Alcohol Res Health* 30(1):22-27 (PMID: 17718397)
  * Goedde et al. 1992, *Human Genetics* 88:344-346 (PMID: 1733836)

## SLC24A5 (Skin Pigmentation)

### European populations

* **rs1426654 A allele (Thr111)** — 98.7-100% derived allele (near fixation). One of the strongest selection signals in Europeans. Derived allele reduces melanin production.
  * Lamason et al. 2005, *Science* 310(5755):1782-1786 (PMID: 16357253)

### South Asian populations

* **rs1426654** — Highly variable: 78% derived in diaspora populations; higher in Indo-European speakers, lower in Austro-Asiatic speakers. Explains ~27% of skin pigmentation variation in South Indians. Single origin shared with Europeans (identity by descent).
  * Basu Mallick et al. 2013, *PLoS Genetics* 9(11):e1003912 (PMID: 24244186)

### African populations

* **rs1426654 G allele (Ala111)** — Ancestral allele predominant (93-100%). Exceptions due to Eurasian back-migration: ≠Khomani San 32.5% derived; Nama 53.5% derived; Ethiopian Semitic-Cushitic groups 55% derived.
  * Lin et al. 2018, *PNAS* 115(52):13324-13329 (PMID: 30530665)
  * Crawford et al. 2017, *Science* 358(6365):eaan8433

### East Asian and Native American populations

* **rs1426654** — Ancestral allele 93-100%. Light skin in East Asians evolved through **different genetic mechanisms** (OCA2, MFSD12, other loci).

## EDAR (East Asian Marker)

### East Asian populations

* **rs3827760 C allele (Ala370)** — Derived gain-of-function variant with enhanced NF-κB signaling.

| Population | Derived Allele Frequency |
|------------|-------------------------|
| Han Chinese | 87-94% |
| Japanese | 77.5% |
| Korean | 87% |
| Northern East Asians | up to 90% |

Associated phenotypes include **thicker hair** (30% larger cross-sectional area), **increased sweat glands**, **shovel-shaped incisors**, and altered ear morphology. Originated ~30,000 years ago in Central China.
  * Kamberov et al. 2013, *Cell* 152(4):691-702 (PMID: 23415220)
  * Fujimoto et al. 2008, *Hum Mol Genet* 17(6):835-843 (PMID: 18065779)

### Native American populations

* **rs3827760** — 39% (1000 Genomes AMR) to 80-100% in pre-contact/unadmixed populations. Modern frequencies reduced by European admixture.
  * Simons Genome Diversity Project

### European and African populations

* **rs3827760** — Virtually absent (0%).

## CPT1A (Arctic Adaptation)

### Arctic/Inuit populations

* **rs80356779 (P479L)** — Arctic-specific variant affecting fatty acid oxidation; one of the strongest selective sweeps in humans (Fst=0.92).

| Population | Derived Allele (479L) |
|------------|----------------------|
| Nunavik Inuit (Canada) | 95.5% |
| Chukchi (Siberia) | 90% |
| Siberian Eskimo | 87.5% |
| Greenlandic Inuit | 76% |
| Koryaks (Siberia) | 56% |
| Alaska Yupik | ~50% homozygous |

**Near-absence in non-Arctic populations:** Only 2 heterozygotes found in 60,706 individuals (ExAC). Associated with **−2.1 cm height** per allele copy and paradoxically elevated infant mortality despite positive selection.
  * Clemente et al. 2014, *Am J Hum Genet* 95:584-589
  * Fumagalli et al. 2015, *Science* 349:1343-1347 (PMID: 26383953)

## EPAS1/EGLN1 (High Altitude Adaptation)

Three major highland populations (Tibetan, Andean, Ethiopian) evolved **distinct genetic adaptations** to hypoxia—a remarkable example of convergent evolution through different molecular pathways.

### Tibetan/Himalayan populations — EPAS1

* **rs149594770, rs150877473, rs73926265, rs13419896** — Part of a 32.7 kb haplotype that **introgressed from Denisovans** ~48,000 years ago. Tibetans: 72-87% carry adaptive haplotype; Sherpa: similarly high frequency.

| Variant | Tibetan | Han Chinese |
|---------|---------|-------------|
| Denisovan haplotype (AGGAA) | 72% | 2-9% |

The haplotype **lowers hemoglobin** by 0.8-1.0 g/dL, protecting against chronic mountain sickness (excessive erythrocytosis).
  * Huerta-Sánchez et al. 2014, *Nature* 512:194-197
  * Yi et al. 2010, *Science* 329:75-78

### Tibetan populations — EGLN1

* **rs186996510 (D4E)** — Tibetans: 70-85%; Han Chinese: 0.56-0.8%; Andean Quechua: **0% (absent)**. Extreme Fst=0.709 indicates strongest known population differentiation.

* **rs12097901 (C127S)** — Tibetans: ~80%; Andeans: 0.08%. The D4E/C127S haplotype is enriched **>80-fold** in Tibetans compared to Han Chinese.

These variants are **gain-of-function under hypoxia**, promoting HIF degradation and lower hemoglobin.
  * Xiang et al. 2013, *Mol Biol Evol* 30:1889-1898
  * Lorenzo et al. 2014, *Nature Genetics* 46:951-956

### Andean populations

* EGLN1 shows selection signals but via **different regulatory variants** (rs1769793, rs1769792) affecting VO2max rather than the Tibetan coding variants. Andeans maintain **higher hemoglobin** concentrations.
  * Heinrich et al. 2019, *Annals of Human Genetics* 83:171-176

### Ethiopian populations

* EPAS1 and EGLN1 are **not implicated** in Ethiopian high-altitude adaptation. Different genes (CBARA1, VAV3, ARNT2, THRB) show selection signals.

## CREBRF (Polynesian "Thrifty Gene")

### Polynesian populations

* **rs373863828 (Arg457Gln)** — Nearly exclusive to Polynesian populations; essentially absent from global databases (gnomAD: 0.00004%).

| Population | Minor Allele Frequency |
|------------|----------------------|
| Samoans | 25.9% |
| Pukapukans | 24.3% |
| Cook Island Māori | 19.5% |
| New Zealand Māori | 17.4% |
| Tongans | 15% |
| Native Hawaiians | 12.8% |
| Niueans | 9.6% |

**Metabolic paradox:** Each allele copy increases BMI by **+1.4-3.1 kg/m²** but **decreases** type 2 diabetes risk (OR 0.49-0.65). Selection timing (~2,800 years ago) coincides with Polynesian maritime expansion, supporting the "thrifty variant" hypothesis for survival during ocean voyaging.
  * Minster et al. 2016, *Nature Genetics* 48:1049-1054

### Micronesian populations

* **rs373863828** — Present at lower frequencies: Chamorro/Carolinian/Chuukese ~4%.
  * Hanson et al. 2019, *Diabetologia* 62:1647-1652

### Melanesian populations

* **rs373863828** — Very low frequency (2-6%) in Melanesians; **absent** in Papua New Guinea.
  * Naka et al. 2017, *J Human Genetics* 62:847-849

## TAS2R38 (Bitter Taste Receptor)

### PAV/AVI haplotype components

* **rs713598 (Ala49Pro)** — Primary determinant of PTC/PROP bitter taste perception
* **rs1726866 (Val262Ala)** — Intermediate effect
* **rs10246939 (Ile296Val)** — Haplotype component

### African populations (highest diversity)

* PAV frequency: 35-65% (variable across populations)
* AVI frequency: ~35%
* **Rare haplotypes more common:** AAI (~10-22%), AAV present
* Unimodal bitter taste sensitivity distribution (vs. bimodal in non-Africans)
  * Campbell et al. 2012, *Mol Biol Evol* 29:1141-1153

### European populations

* PAV frequency: ~47%
* AVI frequency: ~49%
* **Highest non-taster prevalence:** 31.5% AVI/AVI homozygotes
  * Wooding et al. 2004, *Am J Hum Genet* 74:637-646 (PMID: 15107899)

### East Asian populations

* PAV frequency: ~64-66% (higher than global)
* AVI frequency: ~12-35%
* AAI haplotype: ~20.5% (more common than other non-African populations)
  * Kim et al. 2003, *Science* 299:1221-1225

### Selection evidence

**Ancient balancing selection** maintains intermediate frequencies globally (Tajima's D positive; TMRCA ~1 million years). Both PAV and AVI haplotypes have been maintained, likely for detecting toxic bitter compounds while avoiding unnecessary food avoidance.
  * Risso et al. 2016, *Scientific Reports* 6:25506

## Conclusion

These eleven genes illustrate the powerful role of diet in shaping human genetic diversity. Key patterns emerge: **convergent evolution** produced multiple independent lactase persistence variants across Europe, Africa, and the Middle East within the past 10,000 years. **Population-specific selection** drove dramatic frequency differences in alcohol metabolism genes (ADH1B, ALDH2) and the Arctic-specific CPT1A variant. The FADS cluster demonstrates **opposite selection pressures**—increased LC-PUFA synthesis in plant-based diet populations versus decreased synthesis in the Inuit marine diet adaptation. High-altitude adaptations in Tibetans, Andeans, and Ethiopians represent **convergent evolution through distinct molecular pathways**, with Tibetan EPAS1 variants remarkably derived from Denisovan introgression. The Polynesian CREBRF variant exemplifies a geographically restricted "thrifty gene" that paradoxically increases BMI while protecting against diabetes. Together, these variants provide a molecular record of human dietary adaptation spanning hundreds of thousands of years of evolutionary history.
